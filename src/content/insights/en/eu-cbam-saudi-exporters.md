The EU Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM) puts a carbon cost on certain goods imported into the European Union, based on the greenhouse gas emissions released to produce them, known as embedded emissions. For Saudi exporters of aluminium, iron and steel, fertilisers, cement and hydrogen, the CBAM declaration is filed in the EU by the importer, not by the Saudi plant. But the importer's cost depends on the emissions data the producing installation can supply and have verified. In CBAM's definitive period, when importers pay for embedded emissions through CBAM certificates, the practical work sits with the Saudi producer: monitoring emissions at the installation, calculating embedded emissions per tonne of product and keeping records a verifier can check.

This guide covers who does what, the data EU buyers are likely to request, monitoring methods, verification and a readiness plan. It deliberately does not quote CBAM deadlines, thresholds or certificate prices, which the European Commission sets and updates; check the official sources at the end before planning around a date.

## Who reports under CBAM, and what is the Saudi producer's role?

CBAM places the reporting duty on the EU side, but the information it needs originates at the plant.

| Party | Role under CBAM | What it needs from others |
|---|---|---|
| EU importer (authorised CBAM declarant) | Declares the embedded emissions of imported CBAM goods and surrenders the corresponding CBAM certificates | Embedded emissions per product from the producer, verified where actual data is used |
| Saudi installation operator | Monitors emissions, calculates the embedded emissions of each product and makes the data available to buyers | Metering, production records, supplier data on precursors, an accredited verifier |
| Accredited CBAM verifier | Checks the operator's methodology, data and calculations and issues a verification report | Access to records, staff and, where required, the site |
| Trader or distributor (if any) | Passes emissions data along the chain from producer to importer | One consistent dataset it can forward without alteration |

The key point for a Saudi exporter: you are not filing with the EU, but your data is what the declaration is built on, and a buyer who cannot get usable figures from you has to fall back on other options.

## Which Saudi exports does CBAM cover?

CBAM covers goods in a defined set of sectors, including aluminium, iron and steel, fertilisers, cement and hydrogen. Coverage is set by customs classification, the EU Combined Nomenclature (CN) code of each product, rather than by how a company describes its business. In practice:

- **Check each product line, not the company.** One plant may ship both covered and uncovered products.
- **Look beyond primary products.** Some semi-finished and downstream steel and aluminium products are covered through their CN codes.
- **Account for precursors.** If you make a covered product from purchased materials that are themselves CBAM goods, such as steel billet, aluminium ingot, cement clinker or ammonia, the embedded emissions of those inputs count towards your product. You will need data from those suppliers.
- **Expect scope to change.** EU legislation sets the scope and can amend it, so recheck your product list against current Commission guidance each year.

## What installation-level emissions data will EU importers request?

Expect a structured data request from each EU buyer, often in its own template. The content is broadly the same: what was produced, how, and with what emissions.

| Data item | What it covers | Typical source at the plant |
|---|---|---|
| Installation details | Name, location, operator contact, production processes on site | Site and corporate records |
| Products and CN codes | Each good shipped to the EU with its customs classification | Sales, export and customs documents |
| Production route | Process steps from raw material to finished good | Process flow diagrams, operating manuals |
| Production quantities | Tonnes of each good produced in the reporting period | Production logs, weighbridge records |
| Direct emissions | Fuel combustion and process emissions attributed to each good | Fuel meters, material analyses, continuous monitoring |
| Electricity consumption | Electricity used by each production process and the emission factor applied | Sub-meters, utility bills, supplier data |
| Precursors | Quantity and embedded emissions of CBAM inputs | Supplier declarations and their verified data |
| Specific embedded emissions | Tonnes of CO2e per tonne of each good | Your calculation model |
| Methodology and evidence | Monitoring approach, factors, assumptions and the verification report | Monitoring documentation, verifier output |

Two practical rules help. First, answer every buyer from one controlled dataset, not separate spreadsheets that drift apart. Second, record electricity use per process for every covered product, because for some CBAM goods electricity-related (indirect) emissions count alongside direct emissions; the Commission's sector guidance says which applies to your product.

## How should a Saudi installation monitor its emissions?

The aim is a defensible figure for tonnes of CO2e per tonne of product, usually built from a combination of the approaches below.

### Calculation-based monitoring

Most sources are quantified by multiplying activity data by calculation factors:

- **Standard method:** the quantity of each fuel or process material consumed, such as natural gas, coke, limestone or anodes, multiplied by its emission factor, net calorific value and oxidation or conversion factor.
- **Mass balance:** carbon entering the process in fuels and materials minus carbon leaving in products, by-products and wastes. This suits complex processes such as integrated steelmaking, where carbon moves through many streams.

Accuracy depends on metering and sampling: calibrate flow meters and weighbridges, analyse fuels and materials at a documented frequency, and keep laboratory certificates.

### Measurement-based monitoring

For a well-defined source such as a stack, continuous emissions monitoring systems (CEMS) measure gas concentration and flue gas flow directly. This matters for some non-CO2 gases, such as nitrous oxide (N2O) from nitric acid production at fertiliser plants; aluminium smelting can also emit perfluorocarbons (PFCs). Check whether these gases are in CBAM scope for your product.

### Attributing emissions to products

Define the installation's production processes, then attribute each emission source, precursor and electricity flow to them. Shared utilities such as an on-site boiler or power plant need a documented allocation key. Dividing by production quantity gives specific embedded emissions per tonne.

### Data quality controls

Verifiers look for a controlled data flow, not just numbers:

- a written monitoring methodology describing sources, methods, meters and factors;
- calibration and maintenance records for every meter used;
- procedures for data gaps, such as meter failures, with conservative substitute values;
- version control on the calculation model and sign-off by a named responsible person.

## Actual data or default values: what is at stake?

When verified installation data is not available, an EU importer can use default values published by the European Commission instead. Default values are not built from your plant's performance. If your actual emissions per tonne are lower than the default value applied to your product, only verified installation data can show it, and for a buyer comparing suppliers that figure becomes part of what you sell.

Defaults can still be a reasonable stopgap while your monitoring matures, or for occasional small shipments. Agree with each buyer which approach they will use, and for which shipments.

## How does CBAM verification work for a non-EU installation?

Actual emissions data reported for CBAM must be checked by a verifier accredited for CBAM, not a general sustainability assurance provider. Confirm the accreditation scope directly before signing.

A verifier will typically:

1. Review your monitoring methodology and production processes.
2. Assess the risk of misstatement and plan data sampling.
3. Trace figures from fuel invoices, meter readings and laboratory results to the final per-tonne value.
4. Check attribution between processes, precursor data and the electricity emission factor used.
5. Visit the site where required, to see metering and data handling in practice.
6. Report findings, including misstatements or non-conformities to correct.

Verification looks back over a full reporting period, so records must exist from its start; reconstructing a year of data afterwards is slow and often fails. An internal readiness review before the verifier arrives catches most gaps early.

## A CBAM readiness plan for Saudi exporters

| Step | Action | Output |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Map exposure | List products shipped to the EU, their CN codes, buyers and volumes | CBAM product and buyer register |
| 2. Set boundaries | Define the installation, its production processes and the goods each process makes | Boundary and process map |
| 3. Choose methods | Select a calculation or measurement method for each source and document it | Monitoring methodology |
| 4. Close data gaps | Add or calibrate meters, set sampling plans, request precursor data from suppliers | Gap register with owners and dates |
| 5. Build the model | Calculate direct, indirect where relevant, and precursor emissions per tonne of product | Controlled calculation workbook |
| 6. Test internally | Run an internal review against the checks a verifier is likely to make | Readiness review report and corrections |
| 7. Engage a verifier | Appoint an accredited CBAM verifier and agree scope, timing and site access | Verification plan and contract |
| 8. Align with buyers | Agree the data template, delivery dates and update cycle with each EU buyer | Data-sharing arrangement |

Assign one accountable owner: CBAM data crosses production, maintenance, procurement, finance and sales, and without one it stalls between departments.

## How does CBAM data relate to your GHG inventory, LCA and net-zero plans?

CBAM complements your other climate work rather than replacing it.

- **Corporate GHG inventory.** A Scope 1 and 2 inventory under the GHG Protocol or ISO 14064-1 covers the whole organisation (see our guide to [building a first GHG inventory](/insights/ghg-inventory-scope-1-2-3/)). CBAM needs the same source data organised by installation, process and product, so build both from one data system.
- **Life cycle assessment.** A product footprint under ISO 14040/14044 uses its own system boundaries and CBAM follows its own rules, so an existing LCA is a useful input, not a ready CBAM figure. [Life cycle assessment](/services/life-cycle-assessment/) still shows where emissions concentrate in the value chain.
- **Reduction.** Every tonne of CO2e cut at the installation lowers the embedded emissions your buyer pays for, turning fuel efficiency, process changes and electricity sourcing into commercial levers that a [net-zero roadmap](/services/net-zero-advisory/) can prioritise in line with Saudi Arabia's 2060 net-zero target.
- **Disclosure.** Keep CBAM figures consistent with your reporting under the Saudi Exchange ESG Disclosure Guidelines or IFRS S2; [ESG advisory](/services/esg-advisory/) helps keep one account across reports.
