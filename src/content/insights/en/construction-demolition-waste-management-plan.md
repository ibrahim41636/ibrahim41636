A **construction and demolition waste management plan** is the project document that sets out, before work starts, what waste the project will generate stream by stream, how that waste will be separated and stored on site, which licensed transporters will remove it, how much will be reused or recycled, and how every load will be recorded and checked. It is most useful as part of the project's construction environmental management plan (CEMP), where it gives the developer, the contractor and the supervision consultant one shared basis for managing waste and proving that it was handled correctly.

This guide explains what the plan contains, who is responsible for each part and how it connects to the CEMP. The general principles of waste plans are covered in our [facility waste management plan guide](/insights/facility-waste-management-plan-guide/); this article deals with what is different on a construction or demolition site.

## Why does a construction project need its own waste plan?

A factory produces roughly the same waste every month. A construction site does not:

- **Waste changes with each phase.** Demolition produces concrete, masonry and metals; excavation produces soil and rock; the structure produces offcuts, formwork and concrete washout; finishes produce packaging, gypsum, paint and adhesives.
- **Many parties generate it.** Subcontractors come and go, but the main contractor and the developer remain answerable for where the waste ends up.
- **Space is limited.** Skips and stockpiles compete with materials, cranes and access roads, so the waste area has to be planned into the site layout.
- **Mixing is expensive.** A mixed skip costs more to dispose of, and clean streams such as metals, concrete and cardboard lose their value once they are contaminated.
- **Approvals may require it.** Environmental approval conditions, municipal permits or the developer's contract can include waste requirements that the contractor has to show it meets.

## What does a C&D waste management plan contain?

| Section | What it covers |
|---|---|
| Project and scope | Location, phases, programme, demolition and excavation scope |
| Roles | Accountable person, responsibilities of contractor, subcontractors and consultant |
| Waste estimate | Expected quantities by stream and by phase |
| Hazardous materials | Pre-demolition survey and removal sequence |
| Segregation and storage | Site layout, containers, signage, storage rules |
| Transport and destinations | Licensed transporters and receiving facilities for each stream |
| Reuse and recycling | Targets and routes for each recoverable stream |
| Records | Per-load documentation, waste register, licence copies |
| Monitoring and reporting | Inspections, indicators, corrective actions, monthly report |

### Waste estimation by stream and phase

The estimate drives the number of skips, the size of the waste area and the transport contracts, so build it from project data rather than general rules of thumb:

1. **Demolition:** survey the existing buildings and list materials by type and approximate quantity (concrete, blockwork, steel, glass, timber, fixtures, services).
2. **Excavation:** take cut and fill volumes from the earthworks design and identify how much surplus will leave site.
3. **Construction:** apply wastage allowances to bill of quantities figures for each material, preferably taken from your own completed projects.
4. **Packaging and general waste:** estimate from deliveries, workforce numbers and welfare facilities.

Convert volumes to tonnes using a stated bulk density, record every assumption, and replace estimates with weighed quantities as soon as loads start leaving site.

| Stream | Typical sources | Storage on site | Preferred route |
|---|---|---|---|
| Concrete, masonry, tiles | Demolition, breakouts, rejects | Separate stockpile or skip, kept free of other waste | Crushing for reuse or recycling |
| Excavated soil and rock | Foundations, basements, utilities | Stockpile away from drainage, dampened or covered | Reuse as fill or landscaping where suitable |
| Metals | Rebar offcuts, structural steel, services | Dedicated skip | Metal recycler |
| Timber | Formwork, pallets, crates | Dedicated skip, kept dry | Reuse, then recycling |
| Packaging | Cardboard, plastic film, strapping | Covered bins or balers | Recycling, supplier take-back |
| Gypsum and plasterboard | Partitions, ceilings | Covered, dry container | Separate recycling or disposal route |
| Hazardous waste | Oils, paints, solvents, adhesives, contaminated soil, chemical containers | Bunded, labelled, secured store | Licensed hazardous waste contractor |
| General waste | Site offices and welfare areas | Closed bins | Licensed collection |

### Hazardous materials before demolition

Before any demolition, survey the structure for materials that need specialist handling, such as asbestos-containing materials, old paints and coatings, fluorescent lamps, oils left in plant and tanks, refrigerants in air-conditioning units and stored chemicals. Remove them first. Then follow a selective demolition sequence: soft strip of fittings and finishes, separation of metals and other recoverable items, and only then the structural demolition. This keeps the large inert streams clean enough to recycle.

### Segregation and site layout

Mark the waste area on the site logistics plan, with clear access for collection vehicles. Provide a labelled container for each stream, with signs in the languages your workforce uses, and a separate hazardous waste store with secondary containment. Set out a concrete washout area so wash water does not reach drains or open ground. Keep stockpiles away from drainage paths and site boundaries, and dampen or cover fine material; our article on [dust monitoring on construction sites](/insights/dust-monitoring-pm10-pm25-construction/) explains why stockpiles and haul roads matter.

### Licensed transporters and receiving facilities

Waste should leave site only with a transporter licensed for that waste type, and it should only go to a facility authorised to receive it. Before appointing a transporter, check:

- the licence, the waste types it covers and its expiry date;
- the vehicles that will be used and that loads will be covered;
- the destination for each stream and that facility's authorisation;
- how the transporter will confirm delivery for each load.

Write these points into the contract, including the obligation to return proof of delivery. A [waste management](/services/waste-management/) review of transporters at this stage is far simpler than tracing a load after it has been dumped illegally.

### Reuse and recycling

Apply the waste hierarchy: prevent, reuse, recycle, recover, and only then dispose. On construction projects the most useful measures are usually:

- ordering accurately and using standard sizes and prefabricated elements to cut offcuts;
- reusing excavated material as fill and crushed concrete as sub-base where they meet the specification and approval conditions;
- reusing formwork and returning pallets and packaging to suppliers;
- sending metals, cardboard and clean plastics to recyclers.

Set a target for each recoverable stream at the start, and track the share of waste diverted from landfill. Our [circular economy](/services/circular-economy/) work looks at which streams have a realistic market for recycled material in the project area.

### Records and manifests

Every load needs a record that can be traced from your site to its destination:

| Record | Prepared by | When |
|---|---|---|
| Per-load transfer document (stream, quantity, vehicle, transporter, destination) | Contractor's waste coordinator | Each load |
| Receipt from the receiving facility | Transporter or facility | Returned after delivery |
| Monthly waste register by stream and route | Contractor | Monthly |
| Transporter and facility licence copies, with expiry dates | Contractor | At appointment and on renewal |
| Inspection checklists and corrective actions | Site environmental officer | Weekly |

Reconcile loads that left site against receipts every month. A load without a receipt is a gap that you need to close before an inspector or the developer finds it.

### Monitoring, inspection and reporting

A short weekly inspection should check signage, contamination in each skip, fill levels, the condition of the hazardous store, windblown litter and dust from stockpiles. A monthly report to the developer and consultant should show the quantity of each stream, the diversion rate, hazardous waste quantities, loads still without a receipt and the status of corrective actions.

## Who is responsible for what?

| Party | Main responsibilities |
|---|---|
| Developer or owner | Sets waste requirements and targets in tender and contract; reviews reports; keeps records for the project file |
| Main contractor | Prepares and implements the plan; appoints a waste coordinator; manages subcontractors, transporters and records |
| Subcontractors | Segregate their own waste in the containers provided; follow site rules |
| Supervision consultant | Reviews the plan before mobilisation; inspects and verifies records during the works |
| Licensed transporter | Collects only the waste types it is licensed for; delivers to the agreed facility; returns proof of delivery |
| Receiving facility | Accepts waste within its authorisation; confirms receipt |

## How does the waste plan link to the CEMP?

A construction environmental management plan (CEMP) is the overall plan for controlling a project's environmental effects during construction: dust, noise, water, waste, hazardous materials, traffic and complaints. The C&D waste management plan is usually one of its sub-plans or annexes, so the two must agree:

- **Site layout:** the waste area, hazardous store and washout area shown in the CEMP drawings.
- **Approval conditions:** waste-related conditions in the project's environmental approval carried into the waste plan.
- **Inspections:** waste checks included in the CEMP inspection schedule.
- **Training:** waste segregation included in induction and toolbox talks.
- **Incidents:** spills and illegal dumping handled through the CEMP incident procedure.
- **Reporting:** waste indicators included in the CEMP monthly report.

If you are preparing or updating a CEMP, our [environmental management plans](/services/environmental-management-plans/) service develops the waste sub-plan alongside the other sections, so the two documents do not contradict each other.

## What should you check in the Saudi regulatory context?

Waste activities in the Kingdom are regulated by the National Center for Waste Management (MWAN) under the Waste Management Law and its regulations. Environmental permits and compliance more generally are overseen by the National Center for Environmental Compliance (NCEC) under the Environmental Law and its Implementing Regulations. Municipal building and demolition permits may also carry conditions on how C&D waste is removed and where it may be taken. Requirements and technical guidance for C&D waste are updated from time to time, so confirm the current position with MWAN, the permitting authority and the conditions of your own approvals before you finalise the plan. Our [environmental compliance](/services/environmental-compliance/) team can map these obligations for a specific project.

## Checklist by project stage

**Before mobilisation**
- Waste estimate by stream and phase completed and assumptions recorded
- Pre-demolition hazardous materials survey done, if there are existing structures
- Waste area, hazardous store and washout area shown on the site layout
- Transporters and receiving facilities checked and contracted
- Plan reviewed by the consultant and briefed to subcontractors

**During works**
- Weekly inspections recorded and corrective actions closed
- Per-load documents filed and receipts reconciled monthly
- Monthly report issued with quantities and diversion rate

**At completion**
- Final waste register and reconciliation
- Lessons learned on wastage allowances for the next project
