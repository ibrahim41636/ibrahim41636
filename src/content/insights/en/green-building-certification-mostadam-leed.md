**Mostadam vs LEED** is a choice between two green building rating systems that answer the same question in different ways. Mostadam is Saudi Arabia's national rating system for residential buildings, commercial buildings and communities, developed for Saudi conditions. LEED, from the US Green Building Council (USGBC), is an international system that multinational tenants and investors tend to recognise. Both score a project on energy, water, materials, site and indoor environment, and both award a tiered rating after an independent review. Neither replaces the Saudi Building Code (SBC), which every project must meet first.

For developers preparing launches for the real estate exhibition season, the practical question is not which label sounds better. It is which one your buyers, tenants and funders will ask about, and whether your design and documentation can support the claim. This guide compares the two systems, shows where the SBC energy requirements fit, and sets out the process, the evidence and the mistakes that cost credits.

## What is the difference between Mostadam and LEED?

Both systems work in a similar way. A project meets mandatory requirements (prerequisites), earns credits for measures that go further, and receives a rating level according to the points achieved. The differences lie in origin, reference context and the audience that recognises each label.

| | Mostadam | LEED |
|---|---|---|
| Developed by | Saudi national rating system | US Green Building Council (USGBC); certification reviews are run by Green Business Certification Inc. (GBCI) |
| Reference context | Saudi climate, resources and local building practice | An international framework applied in many countries |
| Main scopes | Residential buildings, commercial buildings and communities; new projects and buildings in operation | New construction (BD+C), interiors (ID+C), existing buildings in operation (O+M), plus residential and community systems |
| Audience most likely to recognise it | Saudi buyers, national developers and public clients | Multinational tenants, international investors and global hotel and office operators |
| Result | Tiered rating based on credits achieved | Tiered rating based on points achieved |
| Relation to the SBC | Applied on top of code compliance, not instead of it | Applied on top of code compliance, not instead of it |

Level names, point thresholds and the version open for registration change over time. Confirm them on the [official Mostadam portal](https://mostadam.sa/) and [USGBC's LEED pages](https://www.usgbc.org/leed) before you put a target level in a contract or a sales brochure.

## Where does the Saudi Building Code fit?

The Saudi Building Code is the mandatory baseline. It includes energy conservation requirements, and a building must meet them whether or not it seeks a rating. A certificate does not prove code compliance, and code compliance does not earn a rating on its own.

It helps to think in three layers:

1. **Code compliance (mandatory):** SBC requirements, including energy conservation, verified through the building permit and inspection process.
2. **Environmental approvals (where they apply):** larger developments may need an environmental assessment and permit under the Environmental Law and its Implementing Regulations. Our guide to [how environmental impact assessment works](/insights/how-environmental-impact-assessment-works/) explains that process.
3. **Certification (chosen or contractual):** Mostadam or LEED, set by the owner or required by a client, development authority or funder.

In practice the layers share evidence. The envelope, glazing, cooling and lighting decisions that demonstrate code compliance are the same decisions that drive energy credits. One energy model and one set of calculations should serve both, prepared once and kept consistent. Check which code parts apply to your project, and the current edition, on the [Saudi Building Code website](https://sbc.gov.sa/).

## Which projects choose Mostadam, and which choose LEED?

Choose by audience and purpose, not by habit. Five questions settle most cases:

1. **Who will occupy or buy the building?** Saudi homebuyers and local tenants are more likely to know the national label. Many multinational tenants follow global real estate policies that name LEED.
2. **What does the client or funder require?** Read the development agreement, the request for proposal and the financing terms. If a system and level are named, the decision is already made.
3. **What is the asset type?** A residential community, a single tower, an interior fit-out and an existing building in operation each fall under a different rating system within Mostadam and LEED.
4. **Does the owner have a portfolio standard?** Operators and real estate funds often prefer one system across all assets so that their reporting is comparable.
5. **Can the team document it?** Each system has its own evidence format and review route. Choose one that the design team and contractor can document properly.

Typical starting points by project type:

| Project | Usual starting point | What to check |
|---|---|---|
| Residential community or villas for Saudi buyers | Mostadam (residential or communities) | The developer's marketing promise and buyer expectations |
| Grade A offices for international tenants | LEED (BD+C, or ID+C for fit-out) | Anchor tenants' corporate real estate policies |
| Hotel or resort under an international brand | Either | The operator's brand standards |
| Government or semi-government facility | As specified | The contract and tender documents |
| Existing mall, office building or compound | The operations route in either system | Quality of metered data and the operating budget |

Dual certification is possible, but it repeats much of the documentation and review effort. Pursue it only when two distinct audiences each require a different label.

## How does certification work, from design to certificate?

The details differ between the two systems, but the path runs through the same stages:

1. **Concept: decide and register.** Agree the system, the target level and the owner's objectives, then register the project. Aim for a margin of credits above the threshold, because some credits will fall away during design.
2. **Schematic design: gap analysis and credit strategy.** Score the design against every prerequisite and credit, give each targeted credit an owner and a budget, and drop the credits the project cannot realistically achieve.
3. **Design development: integrate and model.** Run the energy model and water calculations, test envelope and system options, and select materials. A [life cycle assessment](/services/life-cycle-assessment/) supports material and embodied-impact decisions where the chosen system rewards them. Rooftop [solar energy systems](/services/solar-energy-systems/) and EV-ready parking, covered in our guide to [EV chargers in compounds and buildings](/insights/ev-chargers-compounds-buildings-saudi-arabia/), should also be sized at this stage.
4. **Tender: transfer the obligations.** Write construction-stage requirements into the contractor's scope: waste segregation and records, indoor air quality protection, material submittals and commissioning, each with the evidence format you expect.
5. **Design-stage review, where offered.** LEED new construction projects can submit design credits for an early review before construction; check the equivalent route for Mostadam on its portal. An early review exposes weak credits while they can still be fixed.
6. **Construction: collect evidence as you go.** Track submittals, waste records, photographs and commissioning results every month, not at the end.
7. **Completion: final submission and responses.** Submit the file, answer reviewer comments with evidence rather than explanation, and receive the rating.
8. **Operation: measure.** Compare actual energy and water use with the design targets. For existing buildings, both systems offer an operations route that rewards measured performance.

## What documentation will reviewers expect?

Keep one evidence register, with an owner and a status for every credit. A typical file includes:

- [ ] Project registration details, the owner's project requirements and the agreed credit scorecard
- [ ] Drawings and specifications marked up to show each targeted measure
- [ ] The energy model report, and the SBC energy compliance documents it must agree with
- [ ] Water use calculations, fixture schedules and the irrigation design
- [ ] Material data sheets, environmental product declarations (EPDs) and evidence of recycled or regional content
- [ ] The life cycle assessment report, where material credits rely on it
- [ ] Construction waste records from licensed waste transporters, and site photographs
- [ ] The construction indoor air quality management plan and pre-occupancy records
- [ ] The commissioning plan, test reports and the metering plan
- [ ] For buildings in operation: metered energy and water data for the period the system requires, and operating policies

The common thread is traceability. Every figure in a calculation must lead back to a drawing, a specification or a supplier document that the reviewer can see.

## What are the most common mistakes?

- **Deciding after the design is fixed.** Orientation, glazing and system choices that earn credits are expensive to change later.
- **Choosing the system by habit.** A label your buyers do not recognise adds cost without adding value.
- **Confusing code and certification.** Assuming a rating covers code compliance, or that code compliance earns credits automatically.
- **Value engineering without a credit check.** Swapping a glazing type or a chiller can quietly remove a credit. Route every change through the credit owner.
- **Leaving the contractor out.** Construction-stage credits fail when the tender documents do not say what to record and how.
- **Collecting evidence at the end.** Missing delivery notes and photographs cannot be recreated after handover.
- **Targeting the threshold exactly.** One rejected credit then drops the rating by a full level.
- **Marketing ahead of the result.** "Registered" or "designed to" is not "certified". Brochures and sales material should state the project's actual status.

## What should developers be ready to answer?

When a project goes to market, buyers, tenants and funders may ask:

- Which system and level are targeted, and is the project registered or already certified?
- Has the design-stage review been completed?
- What do the energy and water measures mean for service charges and utility bills?
- How will performance be tracked after handover?

Clear, accurate answers protect the sales team from overstated claims and give the project's sustainability case real weight. [Sustainability advisory](/services/sustainability-advisory/) can align these messages with the owner's wider targets and reporting.
