**Mostadam vs LEED** is a choice between two green building rating systems that reward the same kind of performance but speak to different audiences. Mostadam is Saudi Arabia's national green building rating system, used for residential buildings, commercial buildings and communities. LEED, developed by the US Green Building Council (USGBC), is used internationally and is the label many multinational tenants and investors already know. Neither replaces the Saudi Building Code (SBC), which every project must meet first. As a rule of thumb, start from Mostadam when your buyers, public client or development partner expect the national label, and from LEED when tenants, operators or funders ask for it by name.

For developers preparing launches for this season's real estate exhibitions, the useful question is not which label looks better in a brochure. It is which one buyers, tenants and funders will ask about, and whether the design and its documentation can support the claim. This guide compares the two systems, places the SBC energy requirements in the picture, and sets out the process, the evidence reviewers expect and the mistakes that cost credits.

## What is the difference between Mostadam and LEED?

Both systems follow the same basic logic: mandatory requirements that every project must meet, optional credits for measures that go further, and a rating level based on what the project achieves. Both look at environmental performance across areas such as energy, water, materials, the site and the indoor environment. The differences that matter to a developer are where each system comes from and who recognises it.

| | Mostadam | LEED |
|---|---|---|
| Origin | Saudi Arabia's national green building rating system | Developed by the US Green Building Council (USGBC) and used internationally |
| Typical use in the Kingdom | Residential buildings, commercial buildings and communities | Commercial, hospitality and mixed-use projects, often with international tenants or operators |
| Who usually asks for it | Saudi buyers, national developers and some public clients | Multinational tenants, international investors and global hotel and office operators |
| Who awards the rating | The organisation that runs the system, after its own review | The organisation that runs the system, after its own review |
| Relation to the SBC | Applies on top of code compliance, never instead of it | Applies on top of code compliance, never instead of it |

Rating levels, point thresholds, the versions open for registration and fees are set by each system's operator and can change between versions. Confirm them on the [official Mostadam portal](https://mostadam.sa/) and [USGBC's LEED pages](https://www.usgbc.org/leed) before a target level goes into a contract or a sales brochure.

## Where does the Saudi Building Code fit?

The Saudi Building Code is the mandatory baseline. It includes energy conservation requirements that a building must meet whether or not it seeks a rating. A certificate does not prove code compliance, and meeting the code does not earn a rating on its own.

It helps to think in three layers:

1. **Code compliance (mandatory):** SBC requirements, including energy conservation, checked through the project's permitting and inspection process. Confirm which code parts and which edition apply to your project on the [Saudi Building Code website](https://sbc.gov.sa/).
2. **Environmental approvals (where they apply):** some developments need an environmental assessment and permit under the Environmental Law and its Implementing Regulations, which the National Center for Environmental Compliance (NCEC) oversees. Our guide to [how environmental impact assessment works](/insights/how-environmental-impact-assessment-works/) explains that process.
3. **Certification (chosen or contractual):** Mostadam or LEED, set by the owner or required by a client, development authority or funder.

The layers share evidence. The envelope, glazing, shading, cooling and lighting decisions that show code compliance are the same decisions that drive energy credits. Keep one set of design inputs for the code documents and the rating calculations, and update both together whenever the design changes.

### Is meeting the code enough to market a building as green?

No. The code is the minimum every building must meet. A green claim in sales material needs something a buyer can check: a registered rating target, a certificate once it is awarded, or measured performance after handover.

## Which projects choose Mostadam, and which choose LEED?

Choose by audience and purpose, not by habit. Five questions settle most cases:

1. **Who will occupy or buy the building?** Saudi homebuyers and local tenants are more likely to know the national label. Many multinational tenants follow global real estate policies that name LEED.
2. **What does the client or funder require?** Read the development agreement, the request for proposal and the financing terms. If a system and level are named, the decision is already made.
3. **What is the asset?** A residential community, an office tower, an interior fit-out and an existing building in operation are assessed differently. Check which rating system within each scheme applies to your asset before comparing.
4. **Does the owner have a portfolio standard?** Operators and real estate funds often prefer one system across all assets so that their reporting is comparable.
5. **Can the team document it?** Each system has its own evidence format and review route. Choose one that the design team and contractor can document properly.

### Typical starting points by project type

| Project | Usual starting point | What to check |
|---|---|---|
| Residential community or villas for Saudi buyers | Mostadam | The marketing promise and what buyers expect |
| Grade A offices for international tenants | LEED | Anchor tenants' corporate real estate policies |
| Hotel or resort under an international brand | Either | The operator's brand standards |
| Government or semi-government facility | As specified | The contract and tender documents |
| Existing mall, office building or compound | Either, if the system covers buildings in operation | Quality of metered data and the operating budget |

### Should you pursue both systems?

Dual certification is possible, but most of the documentation and review work is repeated. Pursue it only when two different audiences each require a different label.

## How does certification work, from design to certificate?

The details differ between the two systems, but the path runs through the same stages:

1. **Concept: decide and register.** Agree the system, the target level and the owner's objectives, then register the project. Aim for a margin of credits above the threshold, because some credits will fall away during design.
2. **Schematic design: gap analysis and credit strategy.** Score the design against every mandatory requirement and credit, give each targeted credit an owner and a budget, and drop the credits the project cannot realistically achieve.
3. **Design development: integrate and calculate.** Run the energy and water calculations, test envelope and system options, and select materials. A [life cycle assessment](/services/life-cycle-assessment/) supports material and embodied-impact decisions where the chosen system rewards them. Rooftop [solar energy systems](/services/solar-energy-systems/) and EV-ready parking, covered in our guide to [EV chargers in compounds and buildings](/insights/ev-chargers-compounds-buildings-saudi-arabia/), should be sized at this stage too.
4. **Tender: transfer the obligations.** Write construction-stage requirements into the contractor's scope: waste segregation and records, indoor air quality protection, material submittals and commissioning, each with the evidence format you expect.
5. **Early review, where offered.** Check whether your chosen system allows design-stage credits to be reviewed before construction. Where it does, an early review exposes weak credits while they can still be fixed.
6. **Construction: collect evidence as you go.** Track submittals, waste records, photographs and commissioning results every month, not at the end.
7. **Completion: final submission and responses.** Submit the file, answer reviewer comments with evidence rather than explanation, and receive the rating.
8. **Operation: measure.** Compare actual energy and water use with the design targets. The same data answers buyers' questions about running costs and supports any later rating of the building in operation.

## What documentation will reviewers expect?

Keep one evidence register, with an owner and a status for every credit. A typical file includes:

- [ ] Registration details, the owner's project requirements and the agreed credit scorecard
- [ ] Drawings and specifications marked up to show each targeted measure
- [ ] Energy calculations or the energy model report, and the SBC energy compliance documents they must agree with
- [ ] Water use calculations, fixture schedules and the irrigation design
- [ ] Material data sheets, environmental product declarations (EPDs) and evidence of recycled or regional content
- [ ] The life cycle assessment report, where material credits rely on it
- [ ] Construction waste records from licensed waste transporters, and dated site photographs
- [ ] The construction indoor air quality plan and pre-occupancy records
- [ ] The commissioning plan, test reports and the metering plan
- [ ] For buildings in operation: metered energy and water data and current operating policies

The common thread is traceability. Every figure in a calculation must lead back to a drawing, a specification or a supplier document that the reviewer can see.

## What are the most common mistakes?

- **Deciding after the design is fixed.** Orientation, glazing and system choices that earn credits are expensive to change later.
- **Choosing the system by habit.** A label your buyers do not recognise adds cost without adding value.
- **Confusing code and certification.** Assuming a rating covers code compliance, or that code compliance earns credits automatically.
- **Value engineering without a credit check.** Swapping a glazing type or a chiller can quietly remove a credit. Route every change through the credit owner.
- **Leaving the contractor out.** Construction-stage credits fail when the tender documents do not say what to record and how.
- **Collecting evidence at the end.** Missing delivery notes and photographs cannot be recreated after handover.
- **Targeting the threshold exactly.** One rejected credit then drops the rating by a full level.
- **Marketing ahead of the result.** "Registered" or "designed to" is not "certified". Brochures and sales material should state the project's actual status.

## What should developers be ready to answer at launch?

When a project goes to market, buyers, tenants and funders may ask:

- Which system and level are targeted, and is the project registered or already certified?
- Has any design-stage review been completed?
- What do the energy and water measures mean for service charges and utility bills?
- How will performance be tracked after handover?

Clear, accurate answers protect the sales team from overstated claims and give the project's sustainability case real weight. [Sustainability advisory](/services/sustainability-advisory/) can align these messages with the owner's wider targets and reporting.
