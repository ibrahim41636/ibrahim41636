Developers and investors meet in Riyadh this week for Cityscape Global, and many of the projects on show will move from drawings to site soon after. At that point a familiar question comes up in project kick-off meetings: do we have a **construction environmental management plan (CEMP)**, and who is going to write it?

This guide explains what the plan is, when it is required, and what it needs to contain to be a working site tool rather than a file that sits in a drawer.

## What is a construction environmental management plan?

A CEMP is a project-specific document that sets out how the environmental effects of site works are controlled, monitored and reported, from site setup to handover. It connects what the project committed to in its [environmental impact assessment](/insights/how-environmental-impact-assessment-works/) and the conditions of its [environmental permit](/services/environmental-permitting/) with what actually happens on site every day.

The assessment might say that dust will be controlled. The plan says how: watering haul roads, sheeting trucks, speed limits on site, who checks it and where it is recorded.

## When is a CEMP required?

It is usually needed before site works start, and the requirement can come from one or more places:

- **Approval conditions:** the approval of an EIA or an environmental permit may require a construction EMP and a monitoring programme.
- **The owner or developer:** construction contracts on many large projects require one and review it with the pre-mobilisation documents.
- **The zone operator:** industrial cities and special economic zones set their own environmental requirements for contractors working inside them.
- **Lenders:** projects financed by banks or international institutions are often asked for environmental and social management plans.

There is no single rule for every project, so check what applies before mobilisation.

## What does a CEMP include?

| Section | The question it answers |
|---|---|
| Project description and schedule | What works, where, when and in which phases? |
| Legal register | Which regulations and permit or approval conditions apply to the site? |
| Roles and responsibilities | Who owns each commitment at the owner, contractor and subcontractors? |
| Aspects and impacts register | Which activities could affect the environment, and how significant is each effect? |
| Control measures | What is done to limit each effect? |
| Monitoring programme | What is measured, where, how often, and what happens when an alert level is reached? |
| Training and awareness | How do workers and supervisors know what is expected of them? |
| Emergencies and spills | What happens if fuel or a chemical leaks? |
| Complaints and communication | How are neighbours' complaints received and handled? |
| Inspection, audit and corrective action | How are internal non-conformances found and closed? |
| Records and reporting | What is kept, who receives it, and when? |

## Which environmental topics does it cover?

Details vary by site, but most projects need controls on these topics:

- **Dust and air quality:** watering roads, covering stockpiles and trucks, scaling back work on very windy days, and [dust monitoring](/services/dust-monitoring/) at boundaries near sensitive receptors. Our guide to [dust monitoring on construction sites](/insights/dust-monitoring-pm10-pm25-construction/) explains the methods.
- **Noise and vibration:** working hours, placement of fixed equipment, barriers, and [noise monitoring](/services/noise-monitoring/) near homes, schools and hospitals.
- **Water and drainage:** managing dewatering from excavations, concrete washout water, stormwater, and sewage at labour camps.
- **Soil and hazardous materials:** storing fuel and oils in bunded areas, spill kits on hand, and a clear procedure for dealing with contaminated soil.
- **Waste:** segregation, storage and handover to licensed providers, which a [construction and demolition waste management plan](/insights/construction-demolition-waste-management-plan/) sets out in detail.
- **Natural environment and heritage:** work boundaries, protecting vegetation and wadis, and a stop-work procedure if archaeological finds turn up, where relevant.

## Who writes the plan and who runs it?

- **The owner or developer** sets the requirements in the contract, approves the plan and tracks performance.
- **The main contractor** usually writes and implements it, and appoints an environmental officer on site.
- **Subcontractors** follow the parts that apply to their work and are trained on them.
- **An environmental consultant** writes or reviews the plan, carries out monitoring and audits compliance at regular intervals.

The most common problem is not writing the plan but making sure the people on site actually know it. A one-page summary of the key commitments for each supervisor helps a lot.

## What mistakes weaken a CEMP?

- Copying a plan from another project without describing this site and its surroundings.
- No link between the plan and the actual approval conditions.
- A monitoring programme with no alert levels and no action when they are exceeded.
- Missing records: watering the road is not enough if nobody writes it down.
- Not updating the plan as the works move from excavation to structure to finishing.

A regular [environmental audit](/services/environmental-audits/) finds these gaps before an inspector or a neighbour with a complaint does.
