A facility waste management plan is a documented system for how an operation identifies, handles, stores, transports and disposes of the waste it generates, and how it reduces that waste over time. A good plan protects people and the environment, keeps the facility in line with its approvals, and usually lowers cost. A weak one is a document that sits on a shelf until an inspection exposes the gap between what it says and what happens on site.

This guide sets out what a practical plan should contain.

## 1. Build a waste inventory

Everything starts with knowing what you generate. Walk the site with the people who run each area and list every waste stream:

- **Source:** the process, area or activity that generates it.
- **Description:** what the waste physically is.
- **Quantity:** an estimate per month or per batch, refined later with weighbridge or contractor data.
- **Current route:** how it is stored, who collects it and where it goes.

Do not forget intermittent streams such as maintenance waste, spent chemicals, contaminated packaging, oily rags, sludges and expired materials. These are often the streams that cause problems.

## 2. Classify each stream

Classification decides how each waste must be handled. The first and most important distinction is between **hazardous** and **non-hazardous** waste. Hazardous waste may be flammable, toxic, corrosive, reactive or infectious, and it needs stricter controls at every stage.

Where a waste's properties are uncertain, use safety data sheets, process knowledge and, if needed, laboratory analysis. Record the basis for each classification so it can be explained later.

## 3. Segregate at source

Mixing wastes is expensive. A small quantity of hazardous material in a general waste skip can turn the whole load into hazardous waste, and mixed recyclables often lose their value.

Practical segregation needs:

- clearly labelled, colour-coded containers at the point of generation;
- separate containers for each hazardous stream, compatible with the material;
- short, visual instructions in the languages your workforce uses;
- periodic checks, with feedback to the teams involved.

## 4. Store safely

Storage areas should match the risk of what they hold. For hazardous waste this typically means:

- a designated, secured area with impermeable flooring;
- secondary containment for liquids, sized for the largest container;
- separation of incompatible materials;
- labels showing contents, hazard and start date of accumulation;
- spill kits, fire protection and access for emergency response.

Set maximum storage times and quantities so that waste does not accumulate on site.

## 5. Use licensed transport and disposal

Waste should only leave your site with contractors that are licensed for that type of waste, and it should only go to facilities authorised to receive it. Before appointing a contractor, check:

- their licences and the waste types they cover;
- the final destination of each stream;
- their documentation process for each load.

Keep a copy of the transfer documentation for every load. If something goes wrong downstream, these records are how you show that you acted responsibly.

## 6. Reduce, reuse and recover

Once the basics are under control, the plan should push waste up the hierarchy: prevent it, reduce it, reuse it, recycle it, recover energy from it, and only then dispose of it.

Typical opportunities include:

- returnable packaging with suppliers;
- separating clean cardboard, plastics and metals for recycling;
- recovering used oils and solvents through licensed recyclers;
- process changes that generate less scrap or off-spec product.

Each opportunity should have an owner, an expected benefit and a date.

## 7. Keep records and track KPIs

Records turn the plan into evidence. Maintain:

- the waste inventory and classifications;
- monthly quantities per stream and per route;
- transfer documents and disposal certificates;
- contractor licences and their expiry dates;
- inspection checklists and corrective actions.

A small set of KPIs is enough to show progress: total waste generated, share of hazardous waste, recycling and recovery rate, and cost per tonne.

## 8. Assign responsibilities and train

Name the person accountable for the plan and the people responsible for each area. Train new staff and contractors on segregation and spill response, and repeat the training when procedures change.
