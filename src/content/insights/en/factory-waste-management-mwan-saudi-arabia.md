Factories in Saudi Arabia produce many kinds of waste: production residues, used oils, solvents, sludge, contaminated containers and packaging. **Factory waste management** is no longer just housekeeping; it is a regulatory obligation overseen by the National Center for Waste Management (MWAN), with a direct effect on costs and on the facility's reputation.

This guide explains what is expected of a factory as a waste producer, and how to build a system that stands up to inspection and reveals savings.

## What is MWAN's role in factory waste management?

MWAN regulates the waste sector in the Kingdom and applies the **Waste Management Law and its Implementing Regulations**. Its responsibilities include:

- Licensing waste management activities such as collection, transport, storage, treatment, recycling and disposal.
- Setting the requirements that waste producers and service providers follow.
- Tracking waste from its source to its final destination.

This sits alongside the environmental compliance system overseen by the National Center for Environmental Compliance (NCEC), as our guide to [NCEC environmental regulations](/insights/ncec-environmental-regulations-guide/) explains. A factory may answer to both: an [environmental permit](/services/environmental-permitting/) for its activity, and waste management obligations for the waste it produces.

## What is a factory expected to do as a waste producer?

A factory's responsibility does not end when the waste leaves the site. It is usually expected to:

1. **Inventory its waste:** know every waste type, its source and approximate quantity.
2. **Classify it:** decide what is hazardous and what is not, using the approved criteria.
3. **Segregate at source:** keep waste types apart, especially hazardous from non-hazardous.
4. **Store it safely:** suitable, labelled containers in a designated area, for no longer than the requirements allow.
5. **Hand it to licensed providers:** a transporter and treatment or recycling facility licensed for that waste type.
6. **Document everything:** quantity records and transfer documents for every shipment.

## How do you classify factory waste?

Classification is the step everything else depends on. It usually has three stages:

| Stage | Question | Output |
|---|---|---|
| Source | Which process produces the waste? | A waste list per production line |
| Properties | Is it toxic, flammable, reactive or corrosive? | A preliminary classification |
| Confirmation | Is laboratory analysis needed? | A confirmed classification register per waste type |

**A common mistake:** mixing a small amount of hazardous waste into general waste. The whole mixture may then have to be treated as hazardous, which raises transport and treatment costs and regulatory risk.

## How do you choose a transporter and treatment facility?

Choosing a provider is part of your own obligation. Before contracting:

- **Check the licence** with MWAN and confirm it is valid.
- **Check the scope:** does it cover your waste types, especially hazardous waste?
- **Know the final destination:** recycling, treatment or final disposal?
- **Require transfer and receipt documents** for every shipment, and keep them.

## Which records should you keep?

During an inspection, the first question is usually "where are your records?" A good record set includes:

- The list of waste types and their classification.
- Quantities produced and handed over, period by period.
- Documents for each shipment: date, type, quantity, transporter and destination.
- Copies of provider licences and their expiry dates.
- Storage-area inspection records and any spill incidents.

## Where are the savings?

Good waste management is not only about avoiding violations; it shows where you can save:

- **Reduce at source:** adjust a process or raw material to produce less waste.
- **Reuse internally:** containers, or solvents after recovery, where permitted.
- **Recycle:** separate valuable materials such as metals, plastics and cardboard and sell them to licensed buyers.
- **Move waste from landfill to recovery:** a direction supported by national circular economy policy.
