**Solar for factories** in Saudi Arabia, and for warehouses and commercial buildings, makes sense when a large share of the site's consumption happens in daylight hours, there is unshaded space on the roof or within the site, and the system is sized so the facility uses most of its output directly. What settles the question is an analysis of the load profile rather than the roof area, followed by a site survey, a clear view of the grid-connection requirements, and an operation and maintenance plan for a hot, dusty environment.

This guide follows the order in which the decisions are actually made and ends with a checklist for comparing offers on the same basis. For a home or villa, see our guide to [home solar installation cost](/insights/home-solar-cost-saudi-arabia/).

## Is solar worth it for your factory or warehouse?

The useful question is not "how many panels fit on the roof?" but "how much of the panels' output will the site use at the moment it is produced?". The table below summarises the first indicators:

| Factor | Supports feasibility | Needs closer study |
|---|---|---|
| Operating hours | Day shifts or continuous operation | Consumption concentrated at night |
| Type of load | Cooling, air conditioning, compressed air and production lines running in daytime | Intermittent or seasonal loads |
| Shutdown days | Limited downtime at weekends and holidays | Long or frequent shutdowns |
| Space | Large unshaded roofs or spare land on site | Roofs crowded with equipment or shaded |
| Roof condition | Sound roof with long remaining life and enough structural capacity | Old roof due for replacement soon |
| Site tenure | Owned site or long lease | Lease with little time left |

A factor in the last column does not rule the project out. It means the feasibility study has to answer it before the size and budget are fixed.

## What data do you need before a feasibility study?

Gather the following before you approach any provider; it saves time and improves accuracy:

- At least twelve months of electricity bills for every meter on site.
- Interval consumption data (every 15 minutes or every hour), if available from the meter or an energy management system.
- Shift patterns, shutdown days and annual maintenance periods.
- Planned expansions, such as new production lines, EV chargers or switching fuel-fired equipment to electricity.
- The single-line diagram, plus transformer and main switchboard ratings.
- Roof drawings, structural information and age, and any existing waterproofing warranties.
- The site layout and unused areas such as car parks and open land.

If detailed interval data is not available, temporary loggers can be installed on the main switchboard for a few weeks of normal operation.

## How do you read a load profile?

A load profile is the curve of a facility's consumption across the day, the week and the year, and it is the most important input for sizing. Focus on four questions:

1. **What is the daytime base load?** The lowest demand between morning and mid-afternoon on operating days; the site can use this much solar output in full.
2. **What happens on shutdown days?** Panels produce on Fridays and holidays as on working days, so lower demand on those days creates a surplus, and you need to know in advance where it goes.
3. **How does consumption change between summer and winter?** Cooling loads push summer demand up, and solar output also varies by season, so compare the two curves month by month rather than as annual averages.
4. **Are there short peaks?** Compressors or large machines can create peaks that panels alone will not address; this is where storage may enter the discussion.

Two ratios come out of this analysis and drive feasibility: the **self-consumption ratio**, the share of solar output the site uses directly, and the **coverage ratio**, the share of the site's consumption that solar supplies. A larger system raises coverage but usually lowers self-consumption; the right size balances the two against the facility's financial and environmental goals.

## Where should the panels go: roof, canopies or ground?

### Metal roofs on warehouses and factories

- **Structural capacity:** confirm that the frames and purlins can carry the added weight and wind loads, through a documented structural assessment rather than a judgement call.
- **Sheeting condition and age:** a system meant to run for many years on a roof that needs replacing soon will have to be removed and refitted later at extra cost.
- **Fixing method:** clamps fitted to the sheet seams without drilling reduce the risk of leaks; fixings that penetrate the roof need careful waterproofing.
- **Skylights and vents:** exclude them from the layout, and remember that exhaust and extract outlets can soil nearby panels faster.
- **Access and safety:** provide safe walkways for cleaning and maintenance, and agree access and firefighting arrangements with the site safety lead and the competent authorities.

### Concrete roofs

Structures are either ballasted, with no roof penetration, or anchored to the slab with the waterproofing reinstated around each fixing, depending on the roof's load capacity and wind loads. Where air-conditioning plant occupies part of the roof, structures can be raised above it with clearance for maintenance.

### Car park canopies

Canopies turn unused space into generating area and shade vehicles, but they need heavier structures, foundations and a design that accounts for traffic, lighting and drainage.

### Ground-mounted arrays on site

Ground arrays allow more freedom over tilt and orientation and are easier to clean, but they need fencing, drainage and dust control. The larger the area used, the more important early environmental screening of the site becomes, as covered in our article on [environmental studies for solar and wind projects](/insights/environmental-studies-solar-wind-projects/).

## Self-consumption or export to the grid?

Each kilowatt-hour the facility uses directly is worth roughly what it would have paid to buy the same energy from the grid. The value of exported energy, by contrast, is set by the terms of the arrangement approved by the electricity company and the regulator; it may not match the import tariff, and export may not be available for every system size or connection type. So:

- Make self-consumption the basis of the feasibility study, and treat export income as an extra scenario that is relied on only once the current terms are confirmed in writing.
- If export is not permitted or not worthwhile, the system can be fitted with an export-limitation controller that reduces inverter output when demand drops.
- Compare two or three system sizes in the study, and track how the self-consumption ratio and the return change with each step up in size.

This approach means a [solar energy system](/services/solar-energy-systems/) is justified by the facility's own numbers, not by assumptions that may change.

## How does grid connection work?

Connecting a solar system to the electrical network of an operating facility is not a formality, and the requirements vary with system size, supply type and the voltage at the connection point. It can be planned in five stages:

1. **Confirm the counterpart and the applicable arrangement:** check which electricity company serves your site and which regulatory arrangement applies to your system's size and mode of operation, and obtain the current requirements from their official source.
2. **Preliminary technical review:** the connection point, the capacity of existing transformers and switchboards, and the protection system and its settings.
3. **Application file:** the single-line diagram, panel and inverter data sheets with conformity certificates, protection settings and any other documents the electricity company asks for.
4. **Installation:** by a designer and contractor who meet any qualification requirements set by the competent authorities.
5. **Testing and commissioning:** tests and inspection, fitting a suitable meter to measure imported and, where relevant, exported energy, and then permission to energise.

These requirements are updated from time to time. Ask each provider to state which requirements their offer is based on and where they are officially published, who prepares and follows up the application, and whether the meter and any protection changes are included in the price.

## Does a factory need batteries?

Whether [battery energy storage](/services/battery-energy-storage/) (BESS) pays off depends on the job it does. The main purposes at commercial and industrial sites are:

- **Backup for critical loads:** such as control systems, servers and sensitive cooling. Note that a grid-connected solar system usually shuts down during a grid outage unless it is designed for islanded operation.
- **Peak demand reduction:** where the tariff structure or the contracted capacity gives peaks a clear cost for the facility.
- **Shifting surplus to later:** storing surplus from shutdown days or midday hours for use in the evening.
- **Power quality:** for processes sensitive to voltage fluctuation or short interruptions.

Before adding storage, account for fire-safety and ventilation requirements, cooling for the battery room in summer, capacity fade over time and the cost of replacement within the project's life. Storage that does not serve a defined purpose adds cost without a clear return.

## How should you plan O&M for dust and heat?

Heat and dust do not stop a system performing well, but they must be planned for from the design stage.

### Dust

- Set cleaning frequency from measured output loss rather than a fixed calendar, by comparing a regularly cleaned reference row with the other rows, or by analysing how far output deviates from the expected value.
- Watch for soiling sources specific to the facility: process stacks, extract outlets and truck movements on unpaved yards.
- Choose a cleaning method that does not scratch the anti-reflective coating, and define the water source and quality if cleaning is wet.

### Heat

- Panel output falls as cell temperature rises, so leave enough ventilation space beneath the panels and include temperature effects in the yield estimate.
- Install inverters in shaded, well-ventilated locations, because excessive heat can make them reduce output to protect themselves.
- Size cables for the actual temperatures along their route, not standard conditions.

### Monitoring and preventive maintenance

- A monitoring platform that shows output per inverter and raises alerts when output departs from the expected value.
- A performance ratio (PR), the ratio of actual output to expected output under the same irradiance, to track performance over time.
- Periodic thermographic inspection of panels and connections, checks on terminations, and cleaning of inverter filters.

These items belong in a [renewable energy operation and maintenance](/services/renewable-operations-maintenance/) plan with agreed performance indicators and regular reports.

## How do you compare commercial solar offers?

Ask every provider to cover the following items in the same format:

| Item | What to ask for | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| System size | Installed capacity in kWp and inverter capacity | The ratio between them affects output and losses at peak irradiance |
| Expected output | Annual yield in kWh and its assumptions | Irradiance data source, heat, soiling and cable losses, and annual panel degradation |
| Self-consumption | An estimate based on your load data | Shows whether the return is built on your numbers or on generic averages |
| Components | Manufacturer, model and conformity certificates | The basis for technical comparison and warranty claims |
| Warranties | Product and performance warranties on panels, inverter warranty, workmanship warranty | Defines who carries faults and performance loss |
| Roof and structure | Structural assessment, fixing method and waterproofing responsibility | Protects the building, not just the system |
| Grid connection | Who handles the procedure and what is included | Prevents commissioning delays and unexpected costs |
| O&M | Cleaning, monitoring, reporting and spare parts | Determines real output over the years |
| Exclusions | What has not been priced | Reveals the real differences between offers |

Then compare the **expected lifetime cost per kilowatt-hour**: total capital cost plus O&M costs, divided by the expected output over the same period, with the same assumptions applied to every offer. This is a fairer measure than price per kWp alone.

### Warning signs before you sign

- An output estimate without written assumptions.
- A system sized on roof area alone.
- No structural assessment of the roof.
- Unclear responsibility for the grid-connection procedure.
- A financial return that depends on export income whose terms have not been confirmed.
- No O&M plan or monitoring platform.
