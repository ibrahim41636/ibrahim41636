A **construction and demolition waste management plan** is the project document that sets out, before work starts, what waste each phase will generate, how it will be segregated and stored on site, which licensed transporters will remove it, how much will be reused or recycled, and how every load will be recorded and checked. It is usually prepared by the main contractor against the developer's requirements, and it is most useful as a sub-plan of the construction environmental management plan (CEMP), so that waste, dust, traffic and site layout are planned together.

This guide covers what the plan contains, who is responsible for each part and how it connects to the CEMP. The general principles of waste plans for operating sites are in our [facility waste management plan guide](/insights/facility-waste-management-plan-guide/); here we focus on what is different on a construction or demolition site.

## Why does a construction project need its own waste plan?

An operating facility produces broadly similar waste every month. A construction site does not:

- **Waste changes with every phase.** Demolition produces concrete, masonry and metals; excavation produces soil and rock; the structure produces rebar offcuts, formwork and concrete washout; finishes produce packaging, plasterboard, paint and adhesives.
- **Many parties generate it.** Subcontractors arrive and leave, but the main contractor and the developer remain answerable for where the waste ends up.
- **Space is tight.** Skips and stockpiles compete with materials, cranes and access roads, so the waste area has to be designed into the site logistics plan.
- **Mixed waste costs more.** A mixed skip is harder to recycle and costlier to dispose of, and clean metals, concrete and cardboard lose their value once contaminated.
- **Approvals and contracts may require it.** Environmental approval conditions, municipal permits or the developer's contract can set waste requirements the contractor must show it meets.

## What should a C&D waste management plan include?

| Section | What it covers |
|---|---|
| Project scope | Location, phases, programme, demolition and excavation scope |
| Roles | Accountable person on site; duties of contractor, subcontractors and consultant |
| Waste estimate | Expected quantities by stream and by phase |
| Hazardous materials | Pre-demolition survey and removal sequence |
| Segregation and storage | Waste area on the site layout, containers, signage, storage rules |
| Transport and destinations | Licensed transporter and receiving facility for each stream |
| Reuse and recycling | Target and route for each recoverable stream |
| Records | Per-load documents, waste register, licence copies |
| Monitoring and reporting | Inspections, indicators, corrective actions, monthly report |

### How do you estimate C&D waste by stream and phase?

The estimate sets the number of skips, the size of the waste area and the scope of transport contracts, so build it from project data:

1. **Demolition:** survey existing structures and list materials by type and approximate quantity: concrete, blockwork, steel, glass, timber, fixtures and building services.
2. **Excavation:** take cut-and-fill volumes from the earthworks design and work out how much surplus will leave site and how much can be reused as fill.
3. **Construction:** apply wastage allowances to bill of quantities figures for each material, ideally drawn from your own completed projects.
4. **Packaging and general waste:** estimate from the delivery schedule, workforce numbers and welfare facilities.

Convert volumes to tonnes with a stated bulk density for each material, record every assumption, and replace estimates with weighed quantities as soon as loads start leaving site.

| Stream | Typical sources | On-site storage | Preferred route |
|---|---|---|---|
| Concrete, masonry, tiles | Demolition, breakouts, rejects | Dedicated stockpile or skip, free of other waste | Crushing for reuse or recycling |
| Excavated soil and rock | Foundations, basements, utility trenches | Stockpile away from drainage, dampened or covered | Reuse as fill or landscaping where suitable |
| Metals | Rebar offcuts, structural steel, services | Dedicated skip | Metal recycler |
| Timber | Formwork, pallets, crates | Dedicated skip, kept dry | Reuse, then recycling |
| Packaging | Cardboard, plastic film, strapping | Covered bins or baler | Recycling or supplier take-back |
| Plasterboard | Partitions, ceilings | Covered, dry container | Separate route, kept out of inert waste |
| Hazardous waste | Oils, paints, solvents, adhesives, contaminated soil, chemical containers | Bunded, labelled, locked store | Licensed hazardous waste contractor |
| General waste | Site offices and welfare areas | Closed bins | Licensed collection |

### What should happen before demolition starts?

Before any demolition, survey the structure for materials that need specialist handling: asbestos-containing materials in older buildings, old paints and coatings, fluorescent lamps, oil left in tanks and plant, refrigerants in air-conditioning units, and stored chemicals. Remove these first through a licensed hazardous waste route. Then follow a selective demolition sequence: strip fittings and finishes, separate metals and other recoverable items, and only then demolish the structure. This keeps the large inert streams clean enough to crush and reuse.

### How should waste be segregated and stored on site?

- Show the waste area, hazardous waste store and concrete washout on the site logistics plan, with access for collection vehicles.
- Provide one labelled container per stream, with signs in the languages your workforce uses.
- Keep hazardous waste in a separate, secured store with secondary containment and spill kits.
- Contain washout water so it does not reach drains or open ground.
- Keep stockpiles away from drainage paths and site boundaries, and dampen or cover fine material. Our article on [dust monitoring on construction sites](/insights/dust-monitoring-pm10-pm25-construction/) explains why stockpiles and haul roads matter for air quality.

### How do you choose licensed transporters and receiving facilities?

Waste should leave site only with a transporter licensed for that waste type, and go only to a facility authorised to receive it. Before appointing a transporter, check:

- the licence, the waste types it covers and its expiry date;
- the vehicles to be used and how loads will be covered;
- the destination for each stream and that facility's own authorisation;
- how the transporter will prove delivery of each load.

Write these points into the contract, including the duty to return proof of delivery. Our [waste management](/services/waste-management/) team can review transporters and destinations at this stage, which is far simpler than tracing a load after it has been dumped.

### How can C&D waste be reused or recycled?

Follow the waste hierarchy: prevent, reuse, recycle, recover, and only then dispose. On construction projects the measures that usually make the most difference are:

- accurate ordering, standard sizes and prefabricated elements to cut offcuts;
- reusing excavated material as fill and crushed concrete as sub-base, where they meet the specification and approval conditions;
- reusing formwork and returning pallets and packaging to suppliers;
- sending metals, cardboard and clean plastics to recyclers.

Set a target for each recoverable stream at the start and track the diversion rate: the share of total waste, by weight, that was reused or recycled rather than sent to landfill. ### What records and manifests should you keep?

Every load needs a paper or electronic trail from your site to its destination:

| Record | Prepared by | When |
|---|---|---|
| Transfer document or manifest per load (stream, quantity, vehicle, transporter, destination) | Contractor's waste coordinator | Each load |
| Receipt from the receiving facility | Transporter or facility | After delivery |
| Monthly waste register by stream and route | Contractor | Monthly |
| Transporter and facility licence copies with expiry dates | Contractor | At appointment and on renewal |
| Inspection checklists and corrective actions | Site environmental officer | Weekly |

Reconcile loads against receipts every month. A load without a receipt is a gap to close before an inspector or the developer finds it.

### How should the plan be monitored and reported?

A short weekly inspection checks signage, stray waste in each skip, fill levels, the hazardous store, windblown litter and dust from stockpiles. A monthly report to the developer and consultant should show:

- tonnes generated by stream and phase, against the estimate;
- the diversion rate;
- hazardous waste quantities and routes;
- loads still without a receipt;
- open and closed corrective actions.

## Who is responsible for what?

| Party | Main responsibilities |
|---|---|
| Developer or owner | Sets waste requirements and targets in the tender and contract; reviews reports; keeps records in the project file |
| Main contractor | Prepares and implements the plan; appoints a waste coordinator; controls subcontractors, transporters and records |
| Subcontractors | Segregate their own waste into the containers provided; follow site rules |
| Supervision consultant | Reviews the plan before mobilisation; inspects and verifies records during the works |
| Licensed transporter | Collects only the waste types its licence covers; delivers to the agreed facility; returns proof of delivery |
| Receiving facility | Accepts waste within its authorisation; confirms receipt |

## How does the waste plan link to the CEMP?

A CEMP sets out how a project will control its environmental effects during construction: dust, noise, water, waste, hazardous materials, traffic and complaints. The C&D waste plan is usually one of its sub-plans or annexes, so the two documents have to agree on:

- **Site layout:** the waste area, hazardous store and washout shown in the CEMP drawings.
- **Approval conditions:** waste-related conditions in the project's environmental approval carried into the waste plan.
- **Inspections:** waste checks built into the CEMP inspection schedule.
- **Training:** segregation rules covered in site induction and toolbox talks.
- **Incidents:** spills and illegal dumping handled through the CEMP incident procedure.
- **Reporting:** waste indicators included in the CEMP monthly report.

## Which Saudi requirements should the plan reflect?

Waste activities in the Kingdom are regulated by the National Center for Waste Management (MWAN) under the Waste Management Law and its regulations. Environmental permits and compliance more broadly are overseen by the National Center for Environmental Compliance (NCEC) under the Environmental Law and its Implementing Regulations. Municipal building and demolition permits can also carry conditions on how C&D waste is removed and where it may be taken. Requirements and technical guidance for C&D waste are updated from time to time, so confirm the current position with MWAN, your municipality and your own approval conditions before finalising the plan. ## Checklist by project stage

**Before mobilisation**

- Waste estimate by stream and phase completed, with assumptions recorded
- Pre-demolition hazardous materials survey done wherever structures exist
- Waste area, hazardous store and washout shown on the site layout
- Transporters and receiving facilities checked and contracted
- Plan reviewed by the consultant and briefed to subcontractors

**During the works**

- Weekly inspections recorded and corrective actions closed
- Per-load documents filed and receipts reconciled monthly
- Monthly report issued with quantities by stream and the diversion rate

**At completion**

- Final waste register and reconciliation handed to the developer
- Actual wastage compared with the allowances used, for the next project

## Common mistakes on C&D sites

- Writing the plan for the tender and not updating it when the programme changes.
- Letting subcontractors bring their own skips and transporters outside the plan.
- Accepting a transporter's licence without checking which waste types it covers.
- Mixing plasterboard or general waste into concrete and masonry skips.
- Leaving hazardous materials in the building until demolition starts.
