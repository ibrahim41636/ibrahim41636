Noise is invisible, so many projects pay it no attention until the first complaint arrives from a neighbour or an inspector asks for measurement records. At that point the question is simple: do we have numbers we can rely on?

This guide explains how **noise measurement** works at construction sites and facilities, and what a report needs to contain to be useful and defensible.

## What sets noise standards in Saudi Arabia?

The [Executive Regulation for Noise](https://www.mewa.gov.sa/en/Ministry/AboutMinistry/RulesAndConditions/RulesLibrary/Docs/Executive%20Regulation%20for%20Noise.pdf), one of the implementing regulations of the Environmental Law, sets the noise standards that measurements are compared with. It allows those standards to be exceeded at construction sites from 7 am to 6 pm, which means noisy work outside those hours needs planning and measurement.

Alongside the regulation, noise requirements can also come from:

- **The conditions of the facility's [environmental permit](/services/environmental-permitting/)**, at the site boundary or at named receptors.
- **The approval of an [environmental impact assessment](/services/environmental-impact-assessment/)**, which may require baseline measurements before work starts and monitoring during it.
- **The [construction environmental management plan](/insights/construction-environmental-management-plan-cemp/)** and the owner's specifications, which set working hours, measurement locations and alert levels.

Our guide to [NCEC environmental regulations](/insights/ncec-environmental-regulations-guide/) places the noise regulation in context with the others.

## Environmental or occupational noise?

| | Environmental noise | Occupational noise |
|---|---|---|
| The question | What effect does the site have on its surroundings? | How much noise is a worker exposed to? |
| Where it is measured | Site boundary and nearby homes, schools and hospitals | Workplaces and near equipment |
| Reference | Noise regulation and permit or approval conditions | Occupational health and safety requirements |
| Typical action | Scheduling, barriers, relocating plant | Hearing protection, source enclosure, task rotation |

This guide focuses on environmental noise, the kind environmental authorities and neighbours ask about.

## Which indicators does a noise survey measure?

A single number is never enough, because noise changes from moment to moment. Reports therefore usually show several indicators:

| Indicator | What it means | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| LAeq | Equivalent continuous level over the measurement period | The main indicator, usually the one compared with standards |
| LAmax | Highest level recorded in the period | Reveals disturbing events such as hammering and impacts |
| L90 | Level exceeded 90% of the time | Represents background noise without site activity |
| Frequency analysis | How sound is spread across frequencies | Reveals tonal and low-frequency noise from generators and compressors |

Measurements normally use A-weighting, which approximates how the human ear hears sound.

## How are measurement locations chosen?

Choosing the location matters as much as the instrument. The rule is that a location should represent what people actually hear:

- **At the nearest sensitive receptors:** homes, schools, hospitals and mosques.
- **At the site boundary** if the permit conditions set limits there.
- **Away from reflecting surfaces** and unrelated sources such as a nearby air conditioner, unless that is part of the purpose.
- **At the same locations every time**, so that comparisons between periods are valid.

In baseline studies, noise is measured at the same locations before the project starts, so it is clear what the project actually added.

## Short survey or continuous monitoring?

| | Short attended survey | Continuous monitoring |
|---|---|---|
| Format | A technician measures for set periods at several locations | A fixed terminal records around the clock |
| Suits | Baselines, complaint checks, periodic surveys | Long construction projects, facilities surrounded by homes |
| Strength | The technician observes and records the sources | Covers nights and weekends, and sends alerts when levels rise |
| Limitation | Sees nothing outside the measurement period | Needs analysis to separate site noise from other sources |

## How do you make sure the numbers are reliable?

- **Calibrated instruments:** sound level meters with valid calibration certificates, plus a field check with an acoustic calibrator before and after each measurement.
- **Weather:** strong wind and rain push readings up and distort them, so weather is recorded and affected data is excluded.
- **Activity log:** what was running on site at the time? Without it, a reading cannot be linked to its source.
- **External events:** a speeding car or a passing aircraft can raise a reading, so such events are noted and handled in the analysis.

## What does a noise measurement report include?

1. The purpose of the measurement and the requirements it is compared with.
2. A map of the measurement locations and why each was chosen.
3. Instruments, calibration certificates and field check results.
4. Measurement periods and the weather during them.
5. Results tables and charts showing how levels changed over time.
6. Comparison with the applicable standards, showing what falls within permitted construction hours.
7. Recorded activities and external events.
8. Conclusions and recommended mitigation where needed.

## How do you reduce noise on site?

- Schedule the noisiest work inside the permitted hours, and tell neighbours in advance about exceptional work.
- Choose quieter equipment, maintain silencers, and switch off idle plant instead of leaving it running.
- Move fixed plant such as generators and compressors away from receptors, or screen it with barriers and acoustic enclosures.
- Keep a complaints log that links each complaint to the readings and activities at that time, followed by a clear corrective action.
